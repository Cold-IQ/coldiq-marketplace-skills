# Reading the Audience and the Register They Will Accept

The advice to use simple language for non-native and non-technical audiences is correct, but it does
not decide the register. Simple is not the same as casual. A senior professional can want plain
words in a controlled professional voice.

## Work out the register

Ask five questions about the actual recipient:

1. Is the reader a native speaker? This controls vocabulary and sentence length, not formality.
2. How senior is the reader, and how often are they pitched? A heavily pitched reader has less
   tolerance for warmth they did not ask for.
3. What does the profession consider credible? Specialists often trust specificity and evidence.
   Operators want to know what changes in their work.
4. Does the reader control the budget? Do not write to someone as though they can buy when they
   cannot. Write to what they own.
5. How much does the reader already know? An expert needs proof that the sender understands the
   work, not an explanation of the problem.

Record the answer in the strategy brief before drafting.

## Compare two common registers

The same offer can need different writing for a founder and a senior specialist.

| Decision | Founder or growth audience | Senior professional audience |
|---|---|---|
| CTA | A short low-friction question can work | A calm offer or routing line can fit better |
| Last email | A brief close with an easy out | A direct final note with a clear owner question |
| Diagnostic question | Useful | Often the strongest opening |
| Question as CTA | Often acceptable | Can read as needy or too casual |
| Reply menu | Can reduce effort | Can read as a marketing device |
| Warmth | Can create energy | Can read as false familiarity |

These are defaults, not facts about every person. Approved account rules and real reply evidence can
override them.

## Use the right kind of question

Do not ban all questions. Separate them by the work they do.

### Diagnostic question

Allow a question about the mechanics of the reader's operation. It asks something only the reader
can answer. It hands the reader the expertise and proves that the sender understands the work.

Fictional example:

> How much review time goes into the first draft from a new team member?

### CTA question

Use with care for a heavily pitched senior audience. A yes-or-no question about giving time can read
as needy.

Instead of:

> Worth a short call, or should I leave it here?

Use a calm offer:

> I can send the workflow if you want to see it.

### Self-answered question

Ban this in every register. It is a common machine-writing tell.

Instead of:

> The result? Wasted review time.

State the point directly and only when approved evidence supports it.

## Resolve a conflict with general patterns

General campaign patterns often support soft-question CTAs and reply menus. Those patterns can be
useful for founders, growth leads, and operators.

When the reader is a senior professional, the audience decision can override the general pattern.
A pattern that works on average can fail for a specific audience. Record the register in the
strategy brief so the override is deliberate.

## Make clarity outrank cleverness

Every reader is skimming. They must understand each sentence on one pass.

A line that needs decoding has failed even when it is well written. This is the trap: a clever line
can feel like good work and perform worse than a plain one.

Rules that follow:

- Say the direct thing in direct words.
- Name who does what.
- Use no irony, callbacks, or wordplay.
- Make no observations about the reader's career.
- Replace abstract nouns with concrete actions.
- Use the plainer word when one exists.

Fictional comparison:

> Clever: Your early training has come full circle now that new staff depend on your review.

> Clear: You review the first drafts from new staff before the work reaches a client.

The second line says who does what. It does not try to interpret the reader's career.

## Keep claims stable

Register changes do not authorize claim changes. Preserve approved facts and proof exactly. When a
client approves copy word for word, list any tone edit for review before it ships.

Never turn a more formal register into a stronger promise. Never turn a casual register into an
unsupported claim about familiarity.
